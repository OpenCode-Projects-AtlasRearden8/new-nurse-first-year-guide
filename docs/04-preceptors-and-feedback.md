# Preceptors and Feedback

**Education only — not medical advice.**

Your preceptor relationship is the highest-leverage thing in your first six months. This chapter covers what their job actually is, how to take feedback without it landing like an insult, and what to do when the match is wrong.

## Who is who

| Person | Their job | When to use them |
|---|---|---|
| Preceptor | Your day-to-day coach; models the unit | Technique, habits, "how we do it here" |
| Unit educator | Structured learning; competency sign-off | Written gaps, formal skills, policies |
| Charge nurse | Shift logistics; covers when preceptor is off | Real-time help, assignment questions |
| Manager | Hiring, schedule, escalation blockers, complaints | Preceptor mismatch, schedule problems |
| Mentor (informal) | Year-one weather-proofing | "Is this normal?" — anyone 2+ years in, any unit |

## What a preceptor actually wants

Most preceptors teach because they love the craft, and what they want from you is surprisingly short:

1. **Say when you don't know.** Confidence-masking is what actually burns trust.
2. **Act on feedback visibly.** Try the correction next shift without being reminded.
3. **Tell them what you want.** "Can I take the full med pass today?" is a gift to a preceptor — it tells them where to aim.
4. **Be honest about the hard days.** A "rough one today" said at handoff prevents a misread later.

## Feedback is data, not a verdict

Here is the entire skill, compressed: **feedback is information about a behavior, not about a person.**

A loop for receiving any critique:

1. **Thank + capture.** "Thanks — let me write that down" buys your nervous system ten seconds to reset. Actually write it.
2. **Ask for the specific moment.** "When I asked about the drip rate — what did I miss, and what would 'right' have looked like?" Make it about one moment you can replay.
3. **Name your next attempt.** "So next time, I will say the rate back aloud before programming it. I'll try that tomorrow."
4. **(Optional)** Ask for a positive too: "And what's one thing that's improving?" — not for comfort; you need to know what to *keep*.

## Asking for feedback on your terms

Waiting for feedback feels safer and is worse. Two high-yield asks:

- **At the end of week one:** "What's one habit you want me to build, and one thing I do that makes your job harder?" (Aim for the "one thing" — you can only carry two changes at once.)
- **Every Friday (or every third shift):** "Where did I meet the bar today, where did I not, and what's the next stretch?" Three words: the bar, the gap, the stretch.

Log every answer in the [feedback notes worksheet](../worksheets/feedback-notes.html). In week eight you will have a record of being coached — and you will need those receipts the day imposter feelings get loud.

## Preceptor styles you will meet

- **The Narrator** — thinks out loud constantly. Gold. Listen for the "why," not just the "what."
- **The Silent Pro** — watches, rarely comments. Ask directly: "What did you see today?" You will mine more per minute if you make them talk.
- **The Sprinter** — hands you everything fast. "Could we slow down on X?" is your line; they usually respect it.
- **The Coach From Behind** — hangs back, lets you drive. Say "I want you watching my med pass today and picking it apart" to harvest their eyes.
- **The Mismatch** — see below.

## When the match is wrong

Signs — not that they are a bad nurse, but that *this pairing* is not working:

- Feedback arrives as commentary about you as a person ("you're just not a critical care person") instead of about behaviors.
- Teaching time is zero, correction is constant, encouragement is absent — and it stays that way after a direct ask.
- You are hiding things from them, or dreading shifts in a way the shift itself cannot explain (if dread follows *them*, that is the tell).

What to do, in order:

1. **One direct, non-accusatory ask:** "I learn best with a bit more talk-through. Could we do the next med pass with more narration?" Many mismatches end here, honestly.
2. **Talk to your educator or manager** — plainly, without attacking: "I'm concerned the fit isn't working and I'm falling behind on building skills. Could we look at the pairing?" Leaders handle this constantly; it is a normal request, not a betrayal.
3. **Keep it professional to the end.** Year one is smaller than it feels. Never vent about a named preceptor to peers on shift.

## A note if you are your own hardest boss

Some first-years preceptor *themselves* so hard that nobody else could compete. If every critique lands as "maybe I shouldn't be a nurse," try the two-column trick from the [confidence chapter](06-confidence-math.html): facts you would accept from a friend on the left, thoughts-not-facts on the right. Then bring the left column to your preceptor and let the record speak.
