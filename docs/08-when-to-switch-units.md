# When to Switch Units

**Education only — not medical advice.** Career observations, not rules. Your contract, union (if any), and manager own the actual process.

Every first-year nurse eventually asks the quiet question: *is it me, or is it this unit?* Both answers are allowed. This chapter is a sorting tool: how to tell a hard stretch from a wrong fit, and how to move well if you decide to move.

## First: the two questions almost nobody asks straight

1. **Am I running from something, or running to something?** Write both lists. "Running from: 12-hour nights with no breaks" is a real reason. "Running to: days, a step-down pace, a team that teaches" — if the second list is empty, you are not ready to move yet; you are ready to rest.
2. **Which specific part hurts?** "This unit is terrible" is weather, not geography. The pain usually has an address: the schedule, the acuity-to-staffing ratio, a particular team pattern, the lack of coaching, or the specialty itself. Name the address before you file the paperwork — because some addresses change units, and some don't.

## It might be the stretch, not the unit (most common answer)

Year one is hard everywhere. The following are normal and usually *not* a reason to move:

- Feeling underwater through roughly the first 6 months of real assignment. (This is the [confidence math curve](06-confidence-math.html), not a unit problem.)
- One brutal stretch of weeks — several deaths, a terrible preceptor patch, winter census — that **ends.**
- New-unit confusion that resolves with tenure. Nobody knows where anything is in month one; the veterans are useful because they survived that too.
- Fatigue that a real, honest two-week schedule-and-sleep reset repairs ([chapter 5](05-night-shift-survival.html)).

**Test:** if a month of decent sleep, restored breaks, and one recovery weekend would make your unit feel livable, the problem is the load, not the location.

## It might be the unit (also a real answer)

Signals with more weight, especially if they persist after the stretch test:

- **Values mismatch with the unit's floor** — e.g., staffing has a hard floor you don't respect and it does not change over time; travel, patient-ratio, or safety rules are consistently loose.
- **The schedule is structurally incompatible** with your life, and the unit will not flex: permanent rotating flips when your body has told the moon to leave it alone, permanent nights in a season of life that requires days.
- **The coaching really is absent** — no educator to ask, preceptors rotated away in month two, and no plan ever materializes despite direct asks ([chapter 4](04-preceptors-and-feedback.html)).
- **The specialty is simply not yours.** Some nurses love telemetry and hate hemodynamics; some are ED-shaped; some LTC-shaped. Wrong specialty on a great unit is very common and very fixable.
- **Dread does not follow the pattern of stretch.** Stretch dread follows hard weeks and lifts after recovery. Unit dread follows *place* and lifts on days off, in the parking lot, or on transfer rumors.

## The cost side (know it before you leap)

- Year-one experience transfers: assessments, time management, and handoffs mostly. Fast protocols, code roles, and local "we do it here" habits mostly do not — you will be the new nurse again for about 8–12 weeks, and that is fine if you go *informed*.
- Ask early whether your facility allows internal transfer and what it costs: orientation length, seniority, schedule priority, bonus payback if any. (Read your contract and your handbook; this guide is not that handbook.)
- Many nurses change settings within year one and remain excellent nurses. The shame-voice on this topic is loud and wrong.

## If you move, move well

1. **Decide on facts, then feel.** One page: reasons for and against, the address of the pain, what the new unit changes. Write it before you talk to anyone.
2. **Line up the destination first.** Transfers and new employers both; never quit into nothing if you can avoid it.
3. **Tell your manager the true story, politely.** "I've decided to move to X because of the schedule/specialty" is professional and useful to them. Not grievances with names; pattern, not people.
4. **Give real notice, finish strong.** Handoffs clean, per policy; your reputation is your reference.
5. **Plan the new-unit onboarding like your first orientation:** the [worksheets](../worksheets/index.html) apply all over again, including the confidence log — a reset that restarts the count at 1, honestly tracked.

## Questions to ask before you commit anywhere

- Typical ratio on your shift, and how often it holds in reality?
- Orientation length and what "independent" means on this floor?
- Who teaches on an ongoing basis — is there an educator, or is it screensaver veterans?
- Weekend/holiday expectations, and how schedules are actually built?
- What do year-one nurses on this unit *leave* over, and what do they stay for? (Ask two people; the overlap answer is the truth.)

## The takeaway

Staying in a hard stretch is usually the right call and teaches you the most. Moving because the *address* of the pain cannot be fixed where you are is also right. The error in both directions is the same: deciding on a feeling-rich week instead of on two honest lists and one page of facts.
