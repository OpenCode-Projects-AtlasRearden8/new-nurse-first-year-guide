# Organizing Report Notes

**Education only — not medical advice.** Never record protected patient information on anything your employer has not approved. Treat every example below as a blank *template*, not a data entry practice — no patient identifiers, ever, on personal paper.

Your report sheet — your "brain," on some units — is the single highest-leverage tool in year one. A good one turns a chaotic 12 hours into a checklist you can steer. A bad one turns report into a fog you steer blind. This chapter gives you a system simple enough to rebuild from memory at 0659.

## What the sheet has to hold

1. **Who is who** — room, initials per your unit's policy, diagnosis code, code status, allergies, isolation status.
2. **The plan today** — the "why they're here and what's the goal" line in your own words.
3. **The numbers** — latest vitals set, trending labs only if flagged, weights where they matter.
4. **The tasks with clocks** — meds due, dressing changes, rides, deadlines.
5. **The watch-fors** — 2 lines each: what does *worse* look like on this patient, specifically.
6. **Blank space** — you will need more space than you think. Leave 30%.

## A layout that survives the shift

One patient = one block. Keep blocks stacked in priority order, sickest patient at the top — not room order. Within each block:

```
Room / Initials / Dx / Code status / Allergies / Isolation
Plan (one line): why here, main goal today
PMH (short form, only what matters today)
Latest vitals: HR __ BP __ RR __ SpO2 __ T __
Labs of note (flagged only):
Meds/tasks with times:
Watch-fors (2 lines):
Notes space (blank):
```

## The one line that changes everything

Write the **watch-fors** as *decisions*, not vibes. "Watch for," on its own is a reminder to worry. Instead:

> "If SpO2 drops below 92 and stays there for two readings → escalate per policy, get an ABG ask on the table."

> "If BP trends below their usual, recheck manually before calling — their cuff readings run wide."

## During the shift: the four-column add-on problem

Report gives you the morning picture. The shift adds four streams, and they come at you sideways:

- **new numbers** (vitals, labs, I/O): mark with an arrow next to old values so trends pop later.
- **new orders**: write the smoking-gun details exactly as ordered; initial and time-stamp your own sheet per your unit's norms.
- **conversations**: one line + who + time. Family asked about discharge — 10:30. Not for the chart, for *you*.
- **feelings**: a star next to anything that nagged you. At handoff time, the stars are your SBAR bleeders.

## Handoff hygiene

At the end of the shift, your sheet is your handoff source — and what goes in the official record is governed by your facility's policy, not your scrap paper.

- Speak the plan, the watch-fors, the pending tasks, and the emotional cargo ("family is anxious about X — they ask a lot").
- Narrow it: handoff is the six things that change the next 12 hours, not everything you know.
- Visualize your sheet in blocks and go block by block so nothing gets skipped.
- Anything on paper that must survive your shift-ends goes where your employer says it goes, or it does not survive.

## The charge nurse variant

Charge shifts (even informal ones) need a second sheet: an assignment grid — rooms, nurses, patients *rotating*, plus one "traffic lane" list: admissions waiting, rides pending, breaks dashboard. Keep it in your side pocket; it is a shift-long to-do list with weather.

## Two upgrades worth stealing after month two

- **Color habits:** pick two colors only. One for orders/numbers you must not miss, one for "starred" concerns. A third color is how the system dies.
- **Rewrite the top patient's block at midshift.** One line forecast: "where they'll be at handoff." It forces the plan into one sentence.
