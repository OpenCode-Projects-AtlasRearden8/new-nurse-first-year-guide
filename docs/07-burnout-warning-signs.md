# Burnout Early-Warning Signs

**Education only — this chapter is about self-awareness, not about clinical treatment.** It does not diagnose anything. If you suspect you need professional help, that instinct is worth following; a licensed professional is the right tool, and employee assistance programs (EAPs) exist for exactly this.

Burnout in year one is not dramatic. It does not announce itself with a collapse. It arrives as tiny, reasonable-sounding inaugurations of habits that later obviously were the problem. The skill is catching it in the *tiny* phase, when it is still cheap to fix.

## What it is (plain words)

Three ingredients, tested decades of research, in nursing-speak:

1. **Exhaustion** — tired that sleep no longer fixes, the "battery never charges" feel.
2. **Detachment** — patients become items, coworkers become traffic, sarcasm becomes armor 24/7.
3. **"Nothing I do matters"** — the futility voice, applied to work, then to other parts of life.

You will brush against all three some weeks. The warning signs below are the early ones — read them as amber lights, not verdicts.

## Early-warning signs worth acting on within a week

### Body signs
- Sleep is off *pattern*, not just off-schedule: waking at 0400 with a racing mind, needing a weekend to feel human.
- Appetite flips to the unhealthy both ways: no appetite, or feed-coping.
- Getting sick more than your baseline — every cold, every stomach bug, first.
- Headaches, jaw pain, or gut trouble on shift days specifically.

### Emotional signs
- Dread that now starts the night *before* a shift — or two nights before.
- Crying at work or in the car, more than the "this was a heavy one" cry (that one is normal; a pattern is not).
- Cynicism with teeth: jokes about patients that aren't funny even to you.
- Numbness about things that used to matter, at work *and* home.

### Behavioral signs
- Skipping the small self-maintenance you have always done (food prep, laundry, bills) for weeks running.
- Avoiding the floor: taking long detours, calling off, arriving later than you're comfortable with.
- Snapping at people you love over nothing, more than once a week.
- Reaching for relief more often or in bigger servings: alcohol, doomscrolling, shopping, whatever your leak is. (A leak used *occasionally* is human. A leak used as the main relief tool is a signal.)

### Cognitive signs
- Little errors of *reading* — charting the wrong task because you misread the room snapshot.
- Forgetting the *same* thing three times.
- Reading the same paragraph five times. Decisions feel like wading through mud.

**Rule of thumb:** one bad week is life. Two in a row is a flag. Three is a meeting with yourself, and probably a conversation with someone you trust.

## The 2-minute weekly self-check

Run it every Sunday, same time, pen to paper, using the [burnout check worksheet](../worksheets/burnout-check.html). Rate 1–5, where 1 is terrible and 5 is fine:

- Energy: ____ (sleep repairing me: 1–5)
- Mood at work: ____ (dread level reversed: 1–5)
- Detachment: ____ (patients as people, not tasks, reversed)
- Life outside work: ____ (laundry, food, people: 1–5)
- Body: ____ (aches, illness, gut: 1–5)

**How to read it:**

- **Three 1–2 ratings:** run the support plan below *now*, and consider talking to someone professional this week.
- **A declining trend across three weeks:** a real conversation with your manager or educator about schedule and assignment (see [chapter 8](08-when-to-switch-units.html) too).
- **2s living next to a single 5:** one bright spot is still a resource. Move toward it deliberately, not accidentally.

## What actually helps (the boring stuff works)

1. **Sleep first.** Every other intervention is a patch on an empty tank (see [chapter 5](05-night-shift-survival.html) — the schedule *is* the treatment for sleep-shaped burnout).
2. **Pull one plug.** Pick the single most draining non-essential commitment in your life and put it on pause for 30 days. Not everything at once — one plug, then see.
3. **Find the two questions.** "What drained me this week?" and "What filled me?" — one sentence each, in writing. Patterns show up fast; dealing with the pattern beats dealing with the day.
4. **Say the trend, not the crisis.** Talking to a trusted person works better in small sentences than confessional ones: "I'm running low for a few weeks now and I want to catch it early" does more than a breakdown text at 0300.
5. **Use the real help that exists.** EAPs are free and confidential where offered; school and employer health plans have counseling lines; state wellbeing programs exist for nurses. Asking early is the cheap version of asking.
6. **Protect the fun.** The [gaming and chess extras](https://dylanasdale.github.io) exist partly for this: hobbies are not treats, they are maintenance.

## Boundary sayings that hold up

- "No" to extra shifts is a complete sentence *when the schedule is already full*, and especially when you are in the amber zone.
- "I can do that, but not today" is a form of "yes" that protects you.
- The unit survives without volunteers sometimes. It also survives because you *are* sustainable, which is the whole point.

## When it is more than workplace fatigue

If you notice hopelessness that does not lift with rest, drinking or numbing you cannot stop, panic that stops your week, or any thought of harming yourself — those are beyond this guide's job, and they are common enough that help is designed and waiting. EAP, your own provider, the 988 Lifeline (call or text 988 in the US). Same rule as clinical escalation: not sure is decision enough to reach out.

## The takeaway

Burnout prevention in year one is not spa days. It is maintenance math: sleep defended, one plug pulled, two questions answered weekly, trend noticed early, real help used early. The goal is not a nurse who never burns out. It is a nurse who catches the amber lights while they are still cheap.
