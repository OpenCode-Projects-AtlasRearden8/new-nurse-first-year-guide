# Orientation Expectations

**Education only — not medical advice.** Follow your facility's policies and clinical directives above anything here.

Orientation is not a class you pass. It is a ramp. The whole point is that you get to be new, slow, and double-checked while the safety net is visibly there — so that later, when the net is smaller, you already have the habits.

## What orientation actually is

- A supervised runway where someone else still reads over your shoulder.
- Your job during it: build habits, not speed. Speed arrives late and only on its own schedule.
- The quiet deliverable is *judgment*: when to look, when to ask, when to escalate, when to say "this needs another set of eyes."

## The typical shape (rough numbers, every unit differs)

| Phase | What you do | What "good" looks like |
|---|---|---|
| Weeks 1–2 | Shadow fully: observe, take notes, learn where things physically are | Ask good questions; find things quickly |
| Weeks 3–6 | Take a small assignment (2–3 patients) with your preceptor beside you | Safe, honest, improvement visible week over week |
| Weeks 7–10 | Full assignment (or close), preceptor floating/checking | Manage the shift's rhythm yourself; still use the net |
| Final weeks | Solo-ish shifts, preceptor available on call | Independent but not silent — still escalates appropriately |

Hospital orientation is often 6–12 weeks; smaller facilities, clinics, and long-term-care can run shorter. The *range* is normal, so do not compare your length to anyone else's.

## What to expect in week one (so it cannot ambush you)

- You will forget things constantly. Everyone does. Write everything down.
- The EMR will feel like an enemy. It is just unfamiliar. Build shortcuts into your report notes (see [chapter 3](03-organizing-report-notes.html)).
- You will meet about 200 names and remember maybe 12. Fine.
- Time melts. A task that takes 20 minutes in month one will take 8 in month three with zero extra effort.
- The word "competency" gets used a lot. It means a checklist was signed, not that you feel confident.

## Why "expected" is a moving target

Every architect of orientation — educators, preceptors, unit standards — has a different mental picture of your ramp. On some units, week 6 you should "run your assignment solo"; on others, week 12. Both exist. The fix is simple and yours to own:

1. **In week one, ask your educator and preceptor directly:** "What does being ready look like on this unit? What are the specific competencies I'll be checked off on, and what date do they target?"
2. **Write the answers down** in the feedback notes sheet ([worksheet](../worksheets/feedback-notes.html)).
3. **Review it weekly** so you can coach yourself toward visible progress instead of guessing.

## What surprises most first-years

- Orientation feels *short* in hindsight. It is the most supported you will ever be. Milk it: ask the questions now that will feel embarrassing in month four.
- Charting takes longer than patient care at first. Budget real time, and batch when possible per policy.
- You will be given more independence gradually and all at once. The last week of orientation often flips to near-full assignment.
- The emotional load of new nurses is underestimated by almost everyone, including new nurses. Chapter 7 exists for this.

## Self-check questions to ask at the start of orientation

- How long is my orientation and what happens each week?
- Who is my preceptor, and who do I go to when they are off?
- Which codes, assessments, and tasks require a witnessed check?
- What is the escalation policy when I am unsure — who do I call, in what order?
- Where are the policy documents and drug references?
- What does a "general assessment" look like on this unit, in what detail, how often?

## If orientation is going badly

Common pattern: short orientation, weak preceptor match, or you got sick for a week and lost the thread. Each has a fix:

- **Ask for a written list of remaining competencies** and a plan with dates.
- **Escalate the preceptor mismatch politely and early** (see [chapter 4](04-preceptors-and-feedback.html)).
- **Say the sentence:** "I want to make sure I'm safe and not just finished. Can we do one more paired day for X?"

Advocating for more support in orientation is not weakness. It is the cheapest safety tool you own in year one. Nobody will remember in six months that you asked for an extra paired shift; a bad habit learned in a rush can last a year.

## The takeaway

Orientation success is not about being impressive early. It is about being *appropriately dependent* while the net is there, and graduating with habits you can defend: last-10-minutes-of-note taking, verbal read-backs, the same question process every time. Fast comes later. Safe comes first.
